# Effects of Creatine Supplementation on Physical Performance and Overall Health, with a Focus on Female Physiology

## Executive Summary

Creatine supplementation is a well-established ergogenic aid that enhances phosphocreatine stores in skeletal muscles, thereby improving the regeneration of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) for high-intensity, short-duration activities. This leads to significant benefits in physical performance, including increased strength, power, and lean body mass. For women, creatine offers a range of potential benefits beyond athletic performance, including cognitive enhancement, mood improvement, and support for bone health, particularly in postmenopausal individuals. Women generally have lower natural creatine stores than men, suggesting a potentially greater benefit from supplementation. While creatine is generally considered safe for healthy individuals at recommended doses, potential side effects include gastrointestinal upset and water retention. Specific considerations for females include its impact throughout the menstrual cycle and its safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Overall, creatine supplementation appears to be a safe and effective option for many women, with a favorable risk-to-benefit ratio.

## Established Mechanisms of Creatine on Muscle Energy Production and Physical Performance

Creatine plays a pivotal role in the body's energy systems, primarily by enhancing the availability of phosphocreatine (PCr) within skeletal muscles [1, 2]. PCr serves as a readily accessible phosphate donor, allowing for the rapid regeneration of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) from adenosine diphosphate (ADP) through the action of creatine kinase [2, 5, 6]. ATP is the primary energy currency of muscle cells, and its rapid resynthesis is crucial for sustained high-intensity exercise [2, 9].

By increasing intramuscular PCr stores, creatine supplementation augments the capacity of the phosphagen system, which is responsible for providing energy during short, explosive bursts of activity [8, 10]. This enhanced energy buffering capacity allows for increased ATP availability during repeated bouts of intense effort, leading to improvements in training volume and performance [1, 5, 8]. Creatine also functions as an intracellular shuttle, facilitating the transfer of high-energy phosphate bonds from energy-producing to energy-consuming cellular compartments within the muscle [3]. It coordinates energy distribution among various metabolic pathways, including mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, and the phosphagen system [3]. Consequently, creatine supplementation can increase total muscle creatine content by 10–40%, directly supporting faster ATP regeneration and improved anaerobic exercise performance [5, 8, 10].

## Documented Benefits of Creatine Supplementation on Physical Performance

Creatine supplementation has demonstrated consistent benefits across various aspects of physical performance, particularly in activities requiring high intensity and short durations. It significantly enhances strength gains from resistance training, with studies showing greater improvements in both upper and lower body strength compared to resistance training alone [11]. For instance, creatine supplementation during resistance training has been linked to a notable increase in maximal strength, with specific benefits observed in upper and lower body strength metrics [11].

Beyond strength, creatine augments muscle power output and the rate of force development, which is critical for explosive movements like jumping and sprinting [17, 20]. It also improves performance in high-intensity, anaerobic activities by enabling athletes to exert more force and perform at a higher intensity due to enhanced ATP resynthesis [16]. Creatine has been shown to increase peak power, mean power output, and the total work completed during repeated exercise bouts [17]. Furthermore, it can increase total work capacity at near-maximal intensities and extend time to exhaustion in individuals performing intermittent high-intensity exercise [19].

Creatine is also recognized as a highly effective supplement for increasing lean body mass (LBM) when combined with resistance training, often leading to greater gains than training alone [12]. While it does not significantly impact aerobic endurance, it can enhance functionality, strength, and delay fatigue, even in older adults [13, 21, 22]. Creatine supplementation can also aid in the maintenance of muscle mass, strength, and endurance during periods of inactivity and promote faster recovery from exercise-induced muscle damage [15, 22].

## Potential Health Risks and Side Effects of Creatine Supplementation (General Population)

For healthy individuals, creatine supplementation is generally considered safe when taken at recommended doses, typically 3-5 grams per day [25, 30, 32]. However, some individuals may experience side effects, most commonly gastrointestinal disturbances such as bloating, cramps, diarrhea, or nausea [25, 27]. These issues are often more prevalent during the loading phase when higher doses are consumed rapidly and can sometimes be mitigated by dividing the daily dose or consuming it with food [4, 27].

Weight gain, primarily due to increased water retention within muscle cells, is another commonly reported effect [4, 28, 29]. While creatine can slightly elevate blood creatinine levels, a marker often used in kidney function tests, this elevation does not typically indicate kidney damage or dysfunction in healthy individuals [31, 32]. Studies have not supported a link between creatine supplementation and hair loss, dehydration, or muscle cramping when taken at recommended dosages [22].

Individuals with pre-existing kidney disease, diabetes with kidney involvement, or those taking medications that affect kidney function should consult a healthcare provider before using creatine [25, 32]. Long-term safety data for creatine supplements is extensive, with studies showing no significant detrimental effects even with high doses (up to 30 g/day) for up to 5 years in healthy adults [31, 32]. Pregnant or nursing women, and individuals under 18, are generally advised against creatine use due to a lack of comprehensive safety data in these populations [29].

## Creatine Supplementation's Impact on Female Physiology and Physical Performance

Creatine supplementation can elicit significant physiological adaptations in females, including increased muscle mass, strength, and muscle fiber hypertrophy, by enhancing intramuscular PCr stores [34]. This can lead to a greater training stimulus and improved recovery between exercise sessions [34]. Research indicates that creatine may benefit both anaerobic and aerobic exercise performance in women, potentially aiding in faster recovery between competitive events or on successive days [34].

Women generally possess lower natural muscle creatine stores compared to men, which may make them more responsive to supplementation [41, 63]. This suggests that creatine can be particularly beneficial for women in improving strength, endurance, and functional performance [41]. While some studies have shown a decreased response in women possibly due to higher baseline stores, the prevailing evidence suggests that women can benefit from supplementation to increase these stores [36].

Creatine supplementation in women has been demonstrated to enhance power markers, such as 1RM bench press and vertical jump performance [36]. It can promote lean muscle growth and help mitigate age-related muscle loss, countering the misconception that it causes excessive bulkiness [39]. Creatine supplementation can also improve workout energy, support muscle recovery, and potentially aid cognitive function in women [41]. While one study noted no significant increase in chest press strength for females compared to males on creatine, the overall benefits for strength and muscle growth when combined with training are supported [42].

## Documented Health Benefits of Creatine Supplementation for Female Physiology and Well-being

Beyond athletic performance, creatine supplementation offers several health benefits specifically relevant to women. It can enhance muscle strength, exercise performance, and body composition, especially when integrated with resistance training [46, 47]. Creatine may also provide cognitive and mood benefits, potentially alleviating symptoms of depression and improving brain function [46, 47]. Studies suggest that women may have lower baseline creatine levels in the brain, particularly in the frontal lobe, which is crucial for mood, cognition, memory, and emotion, making supplementation potentially more impactful in these areas [48].

Creatine supplementation has shown strong evidence for improving mood and reducing depression symptoms in women [48, 50, 57]. It appears to enhance cognitive performance and reduce mental fatigue, particularly during periods of stress, such as sleep deprivation [48, 50, 62]. Emerging evidence also suggests benefits during pregnancy and post-menopause [47]. For postmenopausal women, creatine can be particularly beneficial for maintaining muscle mass and strength, supporting bone health, and enhancing cognitive function and mood, especially when combined with resistance training [46, 50].

In younger, premenopausal women who exercise, creatine has been shown to improve strength and exercise performance [35]. It can also help alleviate fatigue-related symptoms associated with the menstrual cycle, potentially by supporting brain energy levels and impacting fluid balance [51, 58]. Creatine supplementation may also contribute to improved sleep quality, particularly in perimenopausal women and younger women experiencing insufficient sleep [53]. Furthermore, consuming a creatine-rich diet has been associated with a lower risk of reproductive issues in women [65].

## Potential Health Risks and Side Effects of Creatine Supplementation Specific to Female Physiology

While creatine is generally safe for women, certain considerations are specific to female anatomy and physiology. Hormonal fluctuations throughout the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause can influence creatine metabolism and its effects [35, 46]. During the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, hormonal changes can affect fluid balance and cellular energy availability, potentially increasing the risk of dehydration or impacting thermoregulation, though creatine may help mitigate some of these effects by promoting intracellular water retention [35, 58].

Pregnant women and breastfeeding mothers should avoid creatine supplementation due to a lack of comprehensive safety data in these populations [54]. While older concerns about kidney function have been largely debunked for healthy individuals, those with pre-existing kidney conditions or related health issues should exercise caution and consult a doctor [55].

Creatine supplementation throughout the menstrual cycle may help reduce negative impacts on cognition and sleep, particularly when estrogen levels are low, as creatine kinase activity can be minimal during these times [34, 57]. Some research suggests that consuming creatine with a large amount of carbohydrates might lead to weight gain, which may not be an optimal strategy for all females [34]. However, creatine does not typically lead to significant body mass increase or a bulky appearance in women; any perceived weight gain is often attributed to increased intracellular fluid and lean mass [35, 52, 63, 64]. Bloating and gastrointestinal issues are common early side effects but tend to resolve as the body adapts [52]. Overall, creatine supplementation has not been shown to cause adverse effects on the gastrointestinal, renal, hepatic, or cardiovascular systems in women [34].

## Conclusion

Creatine supplementation is a well-researched ergogenic aid that effectively enhances phosphocreatine stores, leading to improved ATP resynthesis and significant benefits for high-intensity physical performance, including increased strength, power, and lean muscle mass. For women, creatine offers a broader spectrum of benefits, extending to cognitive function, mood enhancement, and bone health, particularly in postmenopausal stages. Given that women generally possess lower natural creatine stores than men, supplementation may provide a more pronounced positive impact. While generally safe for healthy individuals at recommended doses, potential side effects like gastrointestinal discomfort and water retention are possible. Specific considerations for women include its impact during the menstrual cycle and the contraindication during pregnancy and breastfeeding. The existing evidence suggests that creatine supplementation is a safe and effective option for most women, offering a favorable risk-to-benefit profile for both athletic performance and overall well-being.
